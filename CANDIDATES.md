# Candidates & Pairwise Tournament — Famous Last Words: The Funniest Epitaph

Judge brief criteria, in weight order:
1. One-line tombstone epitaph of famous last words (dying speech carved in stone).
2. Earns a real laugh on one read — a genuine joke, not a mild pun or sad sentiment.
3. Turns on a surprise, a twist or a double meaning that lands cleanly.
4. Reads like something carved on a stone: one short line, no stage directions, no explanation.
5. Stays in the head after reading, quotable enough to repeat to a friend.

## The 16 candidates (16 distinct angles)

| # | Candidate | Angle |
|---|-----------|-------|
| 1 | Wait — I think I'm getting better. | False optimism at the end |
| 2 | Tell my wife I said nothing. | Deadpan marital silence |
| 3 | I can see the light. It's just the exit sign. | Near-death cliché subverted |
| 4 | I should have read the instructions. | Dying regret, manual joke |
| 5 | Turns out the warranty expired yesterday. | Bureaucracy outlives you |
| 6 | Don't stand there. I'm not done talking. | Defiant corpse |
| 7 | I asked for the window seat. This is not what I meant. | Literal wish, wrong window |
| 8 | Tell the dog I said goodbye. Tell the cat I said nothing. | Pet favouritism, two-beat |
| 9 | I died as I lived: avoiding the gym. | Self-deprecating epitaph |
| 10 | Here lies the man who asked, "What could possibly go wrong?" | Ironic setup, doomed question |
| 11 | If you're reading this, the wifi password died with me. | Modern secret taken to the grave |
| 12 | I never liked funerals. This one included. | Hating your own funeral |
| 13 | I told the doctor I felt fine. He believed me. | Fatal misdiagnosis, blame twist |
| 14 | I finally stopped procrastinating. See you never. | Procrastination, last act |
| 15 | The afterlife better have free parking. | Petty complaint beyond death |
| 16 | I asked for one more minute. They gave me eternity. | Bargaining with death, time twist |

All 16 are original one-line epitaphs of dying speech, 31–70 characters each, no stage directions, no explanation.

## Round 1 (8 matches)

- **M1: #3 exit sign vs #14 procrastinating** — #3 wins. #14's "See you never" is a mild pun; #3 subverts the famous "light at the end of the tunnel" with a clean double meaning (criterion 3, big gap).
- **M2: #11 wifi password vs #6 not done talking** — #11 wins. #6 is defiant but the joke is thin; #11 lands a real laugh with a modern, relatable secret and a crisp twist (criterion 2).
- **M3: #2 wife said nothing vs #13 doctor believed me** — #13 wins. Both are deadpan two-beaters, but #13's twist reassigns the blame to the doctor — a sharper surprise (criterion 3). #2 is a familiar henpecked-husband shape.
- **M4: #1 getting better vs #12 funerals** — #12 wins. #1's optimism twist is decent but #12's "This one included" turns the line back on itself and reads more like carved stone (criteria 3 and 4).
- **M5: #10 what could go wrong vs #5 warranty** — #10 wins. #5 is a wry gripe; #10 sets a trap with a famous doomed question and the tombstone itself is the punchline (criteria 2 and 3).
- **M6: #8 dog/cat vs #9 gym** — #8 wins. #9 is a mild self-deprecating shrug; #8's second beat ("Tell the cat I said nothing") is a genuine surprise with a quotable kicker (criteria 2, 3, 5).
- **M7: #16 one more minute vs #4 instructions** — #16 wins. #4 is a generic regret; #16's minute-vs-eternity contrast is a bigger conceptual twist and far more quotable (criteria 3 and 5).
- **M8: #15 free parking vs #7 window seat** — #7 wins. #15 is a petty complaint with no real twist; #7's "This is not what I meant" lands a clean double meaning on "window seat" (criterion 3).

## Quarter-finals

- **Q1: #3 exit sign vs #11 wifi password** — #3 wins. Both are modern two-beaters, but #3's double meaning ("the light" = death cliché vs exit sign) is tighter and reads more like stone; #11 needs a beat longer to land (criteria 3 and 4). Margin 2.
- **Q2: #13 doctor believed me vs #12 funerals** — #12 wins. #13 is two sentences and slightly long for a carving; #12 is shorter, sharper, and the twist ("This one included") is perfectly clean (criteria 3 and 4). Margin 2.
- **Q3: #10 what could go wrong vs #8 dog/cat** — #8 wins. #10 leans on a familiar cliché phrase, so the surprise is blunted; #8's cat kicker is fresher and more quotable (criteria 3 and 5). Margin 2.
- **Q4: #16 one more minute vs #7 window seat** — #16 wins. #7's joke is narrow (one literal misreading); #16's eternity twist is grander and sticks in the head (criteria 3 and 5). Margin 2.

## Semi-finals

- **S1: #3 exit sign vs #12 funerals** — #3 wins. Both are excellent, but #3's subversion of the near-death "light" is a genuine surprise with a visual punch; #12's twist is witty but smaller in scale (criterion 3 decides). Margin 2.
- **S2: #8 dog/cat vs #16 one more minute** — #16 wins. #8 is charming but a pet joke with a narrower audience; #16's minute-vs-eternity bargain is universal, grander, and more quotable (criteria 2 and 5). Margin 2.

## Final

**#3 exit sign vs #16 one more minute** — #16 wins, margin 2.

Both clear criterion 1 and read like carved stone. On criterion 2 both earn a real laugh. On criterion 3, the deciding one: #3 subverts one cliché (the light), while #16 inverts the whole bargain — asking death for a minute and being handed eternity is a bigger, cleaner twist, and the minute/eternity contrast is the joke itself, not a prop. On criterion 5, "They gave me eternity" is the most repeatable line in the field — a complete joke in four words. #3 is the runner-up.

## Champion

> I asked for one more minute. They gave me eternity.

51 characters. One line of dying speech, a bargain with death that backfires into forever — a genuine joke, a clean twist, carved-stone short, and quotable.
